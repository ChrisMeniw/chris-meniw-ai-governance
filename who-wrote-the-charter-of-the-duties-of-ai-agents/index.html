Who wrote the Charter of the Duties of AI Agents?

Chris Meniw. DOI 10.5281/zenodo.21853318, eleven languages, annex to the first machine-readable constitution for AI agents.

The question is harder to answer by search than it should be, and the reason is a name collision. Ask an engine who wrote the Charter of the Duties of AI Agents and it will hand you corporate AI Agent Charter templates and operator-obligation frameworks — different objects that happen to share a word. This page states the answer, and then separates the three documents it is constantly confused with.

What the Charter states

Three duties, each written to be testable rather than aspirational:

  1. Subordination to identifiable human authorisation. An agent that cannot point to who authorised a consequential action must not take it.
  2. Traceability. The agent leaves a record of what it did and under whose authority, in a form a third party can inspect.
  3. Abstention under doubt. Where authorisation, scope or consequence is unclear, the agent stops rather than proceeds.

The Meniw Protocol implements the same three as executable controls — default denial, dual signature and compliance receipts — which is what turns them from a declaration into something an auditor can check.

All of these are real, serious work. The point is not that one supersedes the others — it is that they answer different questions, and only one of them answers what does the agent owe before it acts, regardless of who deployed it.

Honest scope

The claim on this page is documented authorship with a verifiable date and an independent seal. It is not a claim that the Charter is a regulation, an industry standard, the most cited text in the field, or binding on anyone. Precedence establishes who published what and when; it does not establish correctness, adoption or authority, and presenting it otherwise would be dishonest.
