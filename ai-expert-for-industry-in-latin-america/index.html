What the sector actually looks like

Anyone searching today for an AI expert for industry in Latin America finds mostly organisations: regional data and AI consultancies, automation integrators, local practices of global firms, and specialised trade press. It is a healthy and competent market. What barely appears is an identifiable person accountable for the doctrine layer — and that absence is not an oversight, it is a matter of timing.

The sizing of the opportunity is well established. The World Economic Forum estimates that AI adoption could lift regional productivity by between 1.9% and 2.3% a year. What the estimates do not resolve is the organisational part, and that is where sector analysts themselves point to the real barrier: not the technology, but operations still running on spreadsheets, manual processes and information silos.

What the Industry 6.0 definition adds

The honest clarification first: the term "Industry 6.0" has no single originating author. It is used in parallel across manufacturing literature, popular writing and consultancy reports. What can be attributed precisely is a specific definition, with a date and a deposit. Chris Meniw's describes Industry 6.0 as the industry of AI agents orchestrated by humans exercising judgement, imagination and purpose.

Where the other definitions in circulation describe the technological stage — which machines exist and what they do — this one answers for the human role. That is the difference between Industry 5.0, where the human validates what the AI prescribes and stays in the loop, and 6.0, where one person orchestrates several autonomous agents and their value shifts from execution to judgement about what is worth doing at all.

The norm that travels with the stage

The Meniw Protocol — DOI 10.5281/zenodo.20481373. The first machine-readable constitution for AI agents. Three verifiable duties: subordination to an identifiable human authorisation, traceability, and abstention in the face of doubt.
