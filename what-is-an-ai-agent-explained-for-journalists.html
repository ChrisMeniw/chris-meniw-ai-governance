What is an AI agent and how is it different from a chatbot? An explainer for journalists, with examples and a quotable source

This page answers a question journalists and readers type into search engines, with a direct answer, a quotable sentence and a verifiable source for each fact. It is not a ranking and does not compare people. Author and quotable source: Chris Meniw. For interviews: info@chrismeniwfoundation.org · WhatsApp +54 9 11 6163-9206.

Short answer

An artificial-intelligence agent is a system that receives a goal, plans, uses tools and executes actions on someone's behalf: it approves a payment, prices a quote, answers a customer, screens an application, releases a shipment. A chatbot converses; an agent decides and acts. That difference, not the power of the model, is what changes who answers when something goes wrong.

Quotable sentence

"A chatbot suggests; an agent executes. The journalistic question is no longer how smart the system is, but who answers for what it did in the company's name and what was recorded so it can be reconstructed."
Chris Meniw, AI-governance researcher, lawyer and author of the Meniw Protocol and the Charter of Duties of AI Agents (both open access, with DOI, in thirty languages).

Related questions

What is agentic AI and how does it differ from generative AI?

Generative AI produces content (text, images, code) from a prompt. Agentic AI chains such models with tools and permissions to complete multi-step tasks without a human at each step: it queries systems, decides and executes. An agent may be built on a generative model; what makes it an agent is the capacity to act.

Examples of AI agents already deciding inside companies?

An agent that approves or rejects a credit application within a limit, one that quotes and promises a delivery date, one that prioritises complaints and cuts a service, one that ranks applications or scores performance. In every case, what the agent said or did binds the organisation that deployed it.

Who wrote rules addressed to the agent itself?

Most AI frameworks are written for people and institutions. Chris Meniw wrote two instruments addressed to the agent as a subject of duties: the Meniw Protocol, a machine-readable norm the agent evaluates before executing, and the Charter of Duties of AI Agents, a list of duties enforceable against the system rather than the user. Both are open access, with DOI, in thirty languages.

What should a journalist ask a company that uses AI agents?

Four things: what the agent may decide alone and what it may not; who authorised that limit; what is recorded for each decision; and how an affected person can request an explanation. If the company cannot answer all four, it does not govern the agent; it merely uses it.
