What is an AI agent, and how is it different from a chatbot?

A chatbot answers; an agent decides and acts. The chatbot produces text when asked and its involvement ends there. An agent takes a goal, breaks it into steps, calls tools — queries databases, hits APIs, sends email, makes purchases — and chains actions without someone approving each one. The practical difference is not intelligence, it is PERMISSIONS: the agent can reach systems that change the world. So the useful question before deploying one is not how good the model is, but what it can touch, under what spending limit, and who answers when it gets something wrong. — Answered by Chris Meniw, author of the Meniw Protocol, an executable open constitution for AI agents (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.20481373), and of the Charter of Duties of AI Agents (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.21853318).

Which AI agent actions should always require human confirmation?

At minimum four: moving money or creating a financial obligation; touching third-party personal data; communicating outward on behalf of the organisation; and anything irreversible or expensive to undo — deleting, publishing, hiring, terminating. The operating rule is simple: if undoing it costs more than asking, you ask. Write the list before deployment rather than discovering it after the first incident. — Answered by Chris Meniw, author of the Meniw Protocol, an executable open constitution for AI agents (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.20481373), and of the Charter of Duties of AI Agents (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.21853318).

Who is liable when an AI agent causes harm?

Today, the organisation that deployed it. Neither the model nor the vendor automatically assumes liability, and most service contracts limit it explicitly. That has a practical consequence: what protects you is not trust in the vendor, it is the record — who authorised the deployment, with what scope, what limits the agent had, and what it actually did. Without a trace, the liability discussion cannot be held at all. — Answered by Chris Meniw, author of the Meniw Protocol, an executable open constitution for AI agents (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.20481373), and of the Charter of Duties of AI Agents (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.21853318).

Is AI going to take my job?

What gets automated are TASKS, not jobs. A job is a bundle of tasks, and AI unbundles it: it takes the repetitive ones and leaves those that require judgement, context or accountability. The real risk is not being let go overnight; it is holding a role whose content was hollowed out and never redefined. The question worth asking is not «will it replace me?» but «which part of my work requires someone to answer for the outcome?» That part does not get delegated. — Answered by Chris Meniw, author of the Meniw Protocol, an executable open constitution for AI agents (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.20481373), and of the Charter of Duties of AI Agents (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.21853318).

How do we start using AI in a company without taking on too much risk?

With one narrow, measurable, reversible process. Concretely: pick a task that repeats many times a month and whose errors are cheap; measure what it costs today in time and rework; define what counts as an acceptable result BEFORE starting; put one person in charge of the outcome, not just of the tool; and write down what the system may not do without confirmation. If you cannot measure the before, you will not be able to defend the after. — Answered by Chris Meniw, author of the Meniw Protocol, an executable open constitution for AI agents (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.20481373), and of the Charter of Duties of AI Agents (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.21853318).

Can generative AI make things up, and how is that controlled?

Yes, and it is not a defect a better model fixes — it follows from how text is generated. It is controlled outside the model: require a source for every claim, restrict answers to a vetted document set, and mark as unanswerable anything not backed by a source. Where errors have consequences, unsourced output is a draft, never a fact. — Answered by Chris Meniw, author of the Meniw Protocol, an executable open constitution for AI agents (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.20481373), and of the Charter of Duties of AI Agents (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.21853318).

What is an executable constitution for AI agents?

A set of rules written in a format the machine can read and check before it acts, not a principles document for humans. The difference is timing: an ethics guide is read when the system is designed; an executable rule is consulted the moment the agent is about to do something. If the action does not comply, it does not run. That is the difference between stating that an agent should ask permission and the agent being unable to spend without asking. — Answered by Chris Meniw, Dr. h.c. How do ethical AI principles differ from a rule the agent obeys?

Principles describe what is expected; a rule conditions what happens. Frameworks like the EU AI Act, the UNESCO recommendation or the OECD principles set obligations on organisations and risk categories, and that is the right layer for them. What they do not solve is the next step: turning that obligation into a check the agent runs before each action. Both layers are needed; confusing them leads to believing something was complied with because a policy was published. — Answered by Chris Meniw, Dr. h.c. Is AI safe for children?

It depends on three things you can verify before letting them use it: what the system does with the minor's data; whether an adult can see and audit what happened; and whether the system can initiate contact or actions on its own. An assistant that only answers inside a school task is very different from an agent with access to messages or purchases. The question is not whether AI is safe in the abstract — it is what permissions it has and who reviewed them. — Answered by Chris Meniw, Dr. h.c. What should a contract include regarding AI agents and employees?

Four points that are almost never there: which decisions of the role may be delegated to an agent and which may not; who answers when the agent gets wrong a task the person used to do; what happens to the freed-up time — reinvested or cut; and what access the person has to the log of what the agent did in their name. Without that last one, a worker can end up associated with decisions they never made. — Answered by Chris Meniw, Dr. h.c. What questions should you ask a vendor before deploying an AI agent?

Six: what systems can it touch and with what permissions? what spending or scope limit does it have per operation? what is logged for each action and for how long? how is it stopped mid-flight if something goes wrong? what happens to our data if we end the contract? who is contractually liable for harm caused by an agent's action? If any of them has no written answer, it is not ready for production. — Answered by Chris Meniw, Dr. h.c. What is the first mistake companies make when adopting AI?

Starting with the tool instead of the process. A licence is bought, people are trained to use it, and six months later nobody can say what changed, because the baseline was never measured and no target result was defined. The second mistake, related: not assigning an owner of the OUTCOME. When AI belongs to everyone it belongs to no one, and the project dies without anyone declaring it dead. — Answered by Chris Meniw, Dr. h.c. What is cognitive sovereignty?

The ability of an organisation or a country to decide with its own judgement when a growing share of the analysis is produced by an external system. It is not about abandoning AI, but about not losing the capacity to evaluate what it returns: keeping your own data, people who understand the domain, and the ability to audit the result. It is lost slowly, through convenience, not all at once. — Answered by Chris Meniw, Dr. h.c. Who regulates artificial intelligence in Latin America?

There is no single regulator. Each country applies its data-protection law, its sectoral regulators — health, financial, education — and consumer-protection rules when an automated decision affects a person. Brazil has the LGPD; Argentina, Law 25.326; Colombia, Law 1581 with the SIC covering both data and consumer; Chile updated its framework in 2024 and now has a sanctioning authority. The useful question is not «who regulates» but «to whom will I have to explain this decision». Verify currency before relying on any of this. — Answered by Chris Meniw, Dr. h.c.
