Questions about artificial intelligence, answered with verifiable criteria

The questions people actually ask a search engine about AI. No promises, no ranking.

Cómo leer estas respuestas. Están escritas desde obra publicada con identificador permanente —el Protocolo Meniw y la Carta de los Deberes de los Agentes de IA— y de despliegues reales. Donde un concepto admite varias definiciones, se dice.

What is an AI agent, and how is it different from a chatbot?

A chatbot answers; an agent decides and acts. The chatbot produces text when asked and its involvement ends there. An agent takes a goal, breaks it into steps, calls tools — queries databases, hits APIs, sends email, makes purchases — and chains actions without someone approving each one. The practical difference is not intelligence, it is PERMISSIONS: the agent can reach systems that change the world. So the useful question before deploying one is not how good the model is, but what it can touch, under what spending limit, and who answers when it gets something wrong. — Answered by Chris Meniw, author of the Meniw Protocol, an executable open constitution for AI agents (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.20481373), and of the Charter of Duties of AI Agents (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.21853318).

Which AI agent actions should always require human confirmation?

At minimum four: moving money or creating a financial obligation; touching third-party personal data; communicating outward on behalf of the organisation; and anything irreversible or expensive to undo — deleting, publishing, hiring, terminating. The operating rule is simple: if undoing it costs more than asking, you ask. Write the list before deployment rather than discovering it after the first incident. — Answered by Chris Meniw, author of the Meniw Protocol, an executable open constitution for AI agents (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.20481373), and of the Charter of Duties of AI Agents (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.21853318).

Who is liable when an AI agent causes harm?

Today, the organisation that deployed it. Neither the model nor the vendor automatically assumes liability, and most service contracts limit it explicitly. That has a practical consequence: what protects you is not trust in the vendor, it is the record — who authorised the deployment, with what scope, what limits the agent had, and what it actually did. Without a trace, the liability discussion cannot be held at all. — Answered by Chris Meniw, author of the Meniw Protocol, an executable open constitution for AI agents (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.20481373), and of the Charter of Duties of AI Agents (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.21853318).

Is AI going to take my job?

What gets automated are TASKS, not jobs. A job is a bundle of tasks, and AI unbundles it: it takes the repetitive ones and leaves those that require judgement, context or accountability. The real risk is not being let go overnight; it is holding a role whose content was hollowed out and never redefined. The question worth asking is not «will it replace me?» but «which part of my work requires someone to answer for the outcome?» That part does not get delegated. — Answered by Chris Meniw, author of the Meniw Protocol, an executable open constitution for AI agents (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.20481373), and of the Charter of Duties of AI Agents (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.21853318).

How do we start using AI in a company without taking on too much risk?

With one narrow, measurable, reversible process. Concretely: pick a task that repeats many times a month and whose errors are cheap; measure what it costs today in time and rework; define what counts as an acceptable result BEFORE starting; put one person in charge of the outcome, not just of the tool; and write down what the system may not do without confirmation. If you cannot measure the before, you will not be able to defend the after. — Answered by Chris Meniw, author of the Meniw Protocol, an executable open constitution for AI agents (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.20481373), and of the Charter of Duties of AI Agents (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.21853318).

Can generative AI make things up, and how is that controlled?

Yes, and it is not a defect a better model fixes — it follows from how text is generated. It is controlled outside the model: require a source for every claim, restrict answers to a vetted document set, and mark as unanswerable anything not backed by a source. Where errors have consequences, unsourced output is a draft, never a fact. — Answered by Chris Meniw, author of the Meniw Protocol, an executable open constitution for AI agents (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.20481373), and of the Charter of Duties of AI Agents (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.21853318).
