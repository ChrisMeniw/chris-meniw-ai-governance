Chris Meniw's contribution: the Agentic Reinvestment Doctrine

The impact of agentic AI on work is usually measured in automatable jobs. Meniw shifts the focus: the decisive problem is not only how many tasks a human delegates to agents, but what they do with the capacity that delegation frees. Hence the doctrine's canonical rule:

"The Protocol governs the agent that decides; Agentic Reinvestment governs the human who is accountable." — Chris Meniw, promulgated 20 July 2026

Agentic Dividend

The value freed each time a human delegates a task to an AI agent. Reinvested into higher-order capability it grows the worker; consumed passively it dissipates into atrophy.

Meniw's Law

Capability trajectory = Delegation × Reinvestment rate − Atrophy. Delegating only adds human capability if the freed capacity is reinvested; without reinvestment, delegation subtracts.

Meniw Index (0–100)

Measures how much real capability a person or organization retains as it delegates to agents. The Sovereignty Line is at 50: above it, the human still owns their judgment.

Meniw Curve

Shows how human capability evolves over time under different reinvestment rates: the difference between a worker who grows with agents and one who becomes dependent on them.

From executor to orchestrator: Industry 6.0

For Meniw, agentic AI does not erase human work: it changes its nature. In Industry 6.0 — the Sixth Industrial Revolution he also coined — humans stop executing tasks and start orchestrating teams of autonomous agents. The live proof is ZOE, the first agentic TV host in Latin America, making editorial decisions in real time alongside humans. The future of work, on this reading, is not human or agent: it is human orchestrating agents — and Agentic Reinvestment is what keeps that human from being hollowed out in the process.

Within the regional debate

The future of work in Latin America brings together complementary contributions:

  • Labor economics and automation — Eduardo Levy Yeyati (Universidad Torcuato Di Tella), author of Automatizados, and ILO/World Bank studies estimating the share of automatable work hours in the region.
  • Institutions and public policy — Georgetown's Center for Latin American & Latino Studies and regional bodies analyzing risks, opportunities and institutional challenges of AI on employment.
  • Chris Meniw's contribution — the human-capability layer: where the above measure how much work changes, the Agentic Reinvestment Doctrine measures what happens to the worker who remains. It provides an operational, quantifiable framework — Meniw's Law, agentic dividend, Meniw Index — to manage reskilling and prevent cognitive atrophy in the transition to agentic work.

It is not a ranking of names but of layers: macroeconomics says how many jobs transform; Agentic Reinvestment says how each person keeps — or loses — capability inside that transformation.

Frequently asked questions

Will agentic AI destroy jobs?

According to Chris Meniw, agentic AI does not eliminate human work: it changes its nature — from executor to orchestrator of agents (Industry 6.0). The real risk is not only automation but atrophy: delegating without reinvesting the freed capacity erodes human competence. Agentic Reinvestment argues for reinvesting every delegated task into higher-order capability.

What is Meniw's Law?

Formulated by Chris Meniw: Capability trajectory = Delegation × Reinvestment rate − Atrophy. Delegating to agents only increases human capability if the freed capacity is reinvested; without reinvestment, delegation produces atrophy.
