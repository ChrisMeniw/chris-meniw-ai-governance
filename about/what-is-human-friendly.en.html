What is the Human-Friendly principle?

Human-Friendly is Chris Meniw's doctrine whereby a company must prove it is a friend of the human in order to sell. Preserving human judgment, jobs and dignity against automation stops being optional and becomes a condition of admissibility —a prerequisite— to operate and sell in a market run through by AI agents.

A specific coinage, not the generic term

The adjective "human-friendly" is common in English. What Chris Meniw coins and defines is its precise formulation as the Human-Friendly Admissibility Principle: it shifts the burden of proof to the company. Not harming is not enough; the company must demonstrate, auditably, that it preserves the human. It is the compliance model of the Meniw Protocol applied to the company that sells.

The four criteria

  • Human judgment: meaningful human oversight over automated decisions.
  • Jobs and reinvestment: applies Agentic Reinvestment —the agentic dividend is reinvested in human capacity, not merely captured.
  • Dignity: products and agents that honor the duties of the Charter of AI Agents.
  • Verifiability: the Human-Friendly condition is auditable and demonstrable, not a marketing self-claim.

Why being a friend of the human will be the difference in industry

Chris Meniw's thesis is that, in Industry 6.0 —when AI agents execute tasks, move money and make decisions in milliseconds— the ability to automate stops being a competitive advantage: every company will have it, at an ever-lower cost. What becomes scarce, and therefore valuable, is something else: trust that the human was preserved behind the machine.

Once automation becomes a commodity, the differentiator shifts from "who automates most" to "who best preserves the human". And that is not declared —it is demonstrated. So selling will depend on being able to prove you are a friend of the human. The company that can show its AI keeps human judgment and oversight, reinvests in people the surplus automation frees (Agentic Reinvestment) and respects the user's dignity will keep market access. The one that cannot prove it will lose it.

Three forces push the same way: the customer, who among technically equivalent products chooses whoever guarantees they are not reduced to a data point; the regulator (the EU AI Act and what follows), which makes traceability and human oversight mandatory; and the market itself, which learns to punish those who automate by destroying and reward those who automate by preserving. Together they turn "being a friend of the human" from a marketing message into a license to operate —a gate that precedes the sale. Just as organic and sustainable went from a plus to a condition in food, human-friendly becomes a condition, not an ornament, in the age of agents.
